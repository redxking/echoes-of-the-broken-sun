People of Soryn

Five lives.
No complete truth.

The war is not driven by heroes and villains. It is driven by people making defensible choices with incomplete information—and discovering that survival can become a reason to control, conceal, or erase.

Character status: These are the principal planned campaign arcs. Mara Vey, Talar Venn, Oruun, and Neme anchor fifteen bounded development operations implemented so far; later relationships, final dialogue, cinematics, and performance remain ahead.
01 / The opening voices

They meet at
the Glass Scar.

One needs certainty, one needs the missing record, and one needs the ground beneath them to survive. The first mission works because all three requests are reasonable and cannot be satisfied without cost.

Meridian Compact / Field commander

Mara Vey

Mara treats uncertainty as engineering debt: leave it unresolved long enough, and someone eventually dies. She prepares carefully, protects evacuation routes, and trusts plans that can survive contact with failure.

Her strength is discipline. Her danger is turning every moral question into a control problem. The campaign tests whether she can protect people without deciding that only one future is safe enough to exist.

Kharuun Assemblies / Memory-bearer

Oruun-of-Seven-Stones

Oruun carries seven mutually correcting accounts of the same evacuation. The name is a chosen relationship to those memories, not a rank or caste. When inherited certainty disagrees with present evidence, Oruun listens to both.

The discovery that an Assembly council removed parts of the ancestral record creates the central question of Oruun’s arc: can a society remain accountable when its continuity depends on curated forgetting?

Archive convoy / Keeper of evidence

Talar Venn

Talar’s first request sounds small beside an evacuation and a collapsing Well: wait long enough to recover a displaced archive convoy. The records matter because whole neighborhoods appear in old census references and nowhere else.

In Names Without Births, Talar follows those absences while the player protects both evidence and civilians. Talar’s storyline asks whether truth still matters when preserving it competes with an immediate life-or-death need.

02 / The larger conflict

The future has
its own witnesses.

The campaign widens from a local evacuation to the political program behind extraction and the consciousness surviving inside unrealized branches.

Meridian Compact / Chancellor

Cael Rhyse

Rhyse is persuasive because his policies have saved cities. He can point to working grids, stocked reserves, and people alive because the Compact imposed measurable limits on uncertainty.

His restoration program aims to secure one stable future for Soryn. It is not senseless cruelty; it is an attempt to make survival governable at an unacceptable existential cost. The harder he is to dismiss, the harder the final choice becomes.

Hollow Choir / Several possible selves

Neme

Neme contains several futures that disagree about whether the Choir can coexist with people from the realized world. Neme sounds precise because careless language may allow one possible self to dominate the others.

This is not a story about uncovering a secret “real” personality. Neme’s arc is a negotiation among genuine internal positions. Mission 14 makes that negotiation a bounded command problem; Mission 15 carries it into one explicit witnessed resolution without claiming that the other positions were never real.

03 / How their paths cross

The campaign is
a chain of arguments.

Characters do not pause to explain the world. They reveal it through requests, evidence, disagreements, and the costs that return in later missions.

Act I

Survival brings them together

Mara, Talar, and Oruun collide at the Glass Scar. A ceasefire under unresolved pressure shows that their assumptions about the Wells are incomplete.

Act II

Their histories stop agreeing

Talar traces erased names, Oruun confronts deliberate omissions, Neme becomes understandable, and Mara must distribute power where not every district can be saved.

Act III / Implemented through Mission 15

The argument becomes a decision

Rhyse presents restoration as a way to save lives. Oruun pursues shared responsibility. Missions 14 and 15 give Neme and the Choir a bounded command perspective and one persisted ending receipt. Final relationships, dialogue, cinematics, and downstream consequences remain unfinished.

The result

No hidden good ending

The ending reports consequences and unresolved costs. It does not turn difficult choices into a single secret morality score.
